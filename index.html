Space Debris Removal Concept

The Magnetic Space Net

A Next-Generation Solution for Space Debris

This project proposes a servicer spacecraft that deploys a flexible net fitted with electromagnets to capture metal orbital debris, then drags the bundle back into the atmosphere to burn up. Below is the background problem, how the concept works, its benefits and risks, and a staged plan to test it. The animation at the bottom is a simple decorative illustration, not a physics model.

The Problem

Why orbital debris has become a growing threat.

The Threat — Kessler Syndrome

Once debris reaches a critical density, collisions trigger a chain reaction that keeps multiplying fragments.

The Scale

About 47,000 objects are tracked from the ground; roughly 68,000 objects are estimated to be larger than 10 cm.

The Cost

An €102 million ESA contract funds removing just one target — the ClearSpace-1 / OHB mission, 2026.

Source: ESA Space Environment Statistics (2026); OHB–ESA ClearSpace-1 debris removal contract.

How the Innovation Works

A four-step capture sequence, based on the real RemoveDEBRIS net demonstration.

  1. Deploy the Flexible Net

    The servicer unfurls a lightweight net ahead of the target.

  2. Electromagnets Activate

    Magnetic coils along the net edges switch on.

  3. Attract Metal Debris

    The field draws conductive fragments onto the net.

  4. Drag to the Atmosphere

    The bundle is lowered and burns up on re-entry.

Reference: RemoveDEBRIS net demonstration, Surrey Space Centre / ESA.

Benefits and Potential Impact

Cost-Effective

A reusable net and magnetic capture can lower the cost of removing each target.

Because the net and magnets can be reused across several passes, a single servicer does not need a brand-new capture system for every target, which keeps future removal missions cheaper.

Scalable

One servicer can collect several pieces of debris during a single mission.

The same servicer can approach one fragment after another in the same orbital band, so a mission can remove more than a single piece before returning to dispose of the collected bundle.

Flexible

Magnetic capture adapts to irregular shapes and slowly tumbling objects.

Many real fragments are broken panels, bent tanks or tumbling scraps with no clean docking point. A net and magnetic field can grip them without a precise mechanical grapple.

Limitations, Risks and Concerns

Kinetic Energy

At orbital speed, a high-speed impact could tear or puncture the net before it closes.

At orbital speed even a small piece carries large kinetic energy; if it strikes the net before the magnets lock on, the mesh could tear and the captured load could escape.

Power Limits

Strong electromagnets demand a large, sustained on-board power supply and thermal control.

Electromagnets strong enough to hold metal at a distance draw steady current, and that power must come from solar panels or batteries, with extra heat that the spacecraft must shed.

Testing Limits

Vacuum, orbital speed and uncontrolled tumbling cannot be fully reproduced on the ground.

Ground chambers can reproduce vacuum and some tumbling, but they cannot reproduce full orbital speed, long-duration operation or the exact space environment, so some risks only appear in flight.

Feasibility and Implementation Plan

A three-step path from laboratory to orbit.

Step 1 · Ground Prototype

Vacuum chamber test — build a ground prototype and prove magnetic capture in a vacuum chamber.

Step 2 · Low-Orbit Test

Small target in orbit — fly a test platform to capture one piece of debris weighing under 5 kg.

Step 3 · Multi-Target Mission

Several objects per flight — scale up to an operational craft that collects multiple targets each flight.

Success metric: capture a target without creating any new debris in the process.

Conclusion and Recommendation

Recommendation: begin by targeting small debris first.

Next action: secure shared international funding and orbital treaties.
